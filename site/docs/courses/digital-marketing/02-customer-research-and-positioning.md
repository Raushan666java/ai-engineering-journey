# Chapter 2 — Customer Research and Positioning

## Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

- Write an ideal customer profile (ICP) that names a trigger event, not just a job title
- Run a problem interview that produces evidence instead of compliments
- Rank evidence into five tiers from opinion to payment, and refuse to build on the bottom two
- Map the job the customer is trying to do, including its functional, emotional and social parts
- Write a positioning statement using the Moore template and test it on a stranger
- Decide between entering an existing category and creating a new one

---

## Theory

### 2.1 The ICP is a trigger, not a demographic

An ideal customer profile is often written as "SMB owners, 25–45, India". That
describes nobody in particular and cannot be acted on, because every decision
after it is a guess.

A usable ICP has four parts:

| part | example | why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **situation** | runs a single-location gym with 200–800 members | defines the context of use |
| **trigger** | just hired a second receptionist, or lost enquiries on WhatsApp | tells you *when* to contact them |
| **current workaround** | owner answers messages personally at 11pm | the real competitor |
| **cost of the problem** | roughly 20–40 missed enquiries a month | the ceiling on your price |

The trigger is the part almost everyone omits, and it is the most valuable. A
trigger converts a permanent maybe into a dated yes. "Gyms" is a market.
"Gyms that just crossed 400 members and are losing enquiries at night" is a list
you can build this afternoon.

The cost of the problem sets your price ceiling before you have written a single
line of the offer. If missed enquiries cost ₹8,000 a month, a ₹25,000 setup plus
₹2,000 a month is defensible. If they cost ₹1,500 a month, it is not, and no
amount of clever copy changes that.

### 2.2 Interviews that produce evidence

Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* reduces to three rules:

1. **Ask about their life, not your idea.** "How do you handle enquiries after
   closing time?" not "Would you use an AI bot?"
2. **Ask about the past, not the future.** What they did last month is data.
   What they say they would do is a politeness.
3. **Ask for specifics, not opinions.** "Which enquiry did you lose most
   recently?" not "Is this a big problem for you?"

The reason is that people are kind. They will tell you your idea is good to end
the conversation. A question about the past is much harder to answer kindly and
much easier to answer truthfully.

A 15-minute interview that works:

```
1. "Walk me through what happens when a customer messages you at 9pm."
2. "What did you do the last time that happened?"        <- past, specific
3. "How many times did that happen last week?"
4. "What did it cost you, roughly?"
5. "What have you already tried to fix it?"
6. "Who else is involved in deciding something like this?"
```

Question 5 is the one that surfaces competitors you did not know about, including
"my nephew made a spreadsheet" and "nothing, we just lose them". Question 6
prevents the most common wasted month: selling to someone who cannot sign.

**What you are listening for:** a number, a name, a date, or an emotional spike.
Anything else is noise. "Yeah, that's annoying" is noise. "We lost the Mehta
wedding booking because nobody replied until Monday" is evidence.

### 2.3 The evidence hierarchy

Not all research is equal. Weight it deliberately.

| tier | evidence | weight | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | they paid money | decisive | a signed contract, an invoice |
| 4 | they committed something costly | strong | a deposit, a calendar slot, an intro to their boss |
| 3 | they did it themselves | strong | built a spreadsheet, hired a person, wrote a process |
| 2 | they described past behaviour in detail | moderate | a specific incident with names and numbers |
| 1 | they said they would | weak | "sounds useful, send me info" |
| 0 | they complimented you | worthless | "great idea" |

The rule: **build on tier 3 and above.** Tier 1 and 2 tell you where to look
next; they do not justify writing code. This single rule prevents the most
expensive mistake available to a technical founder, which is building a working
product for a polite audience.

### 2.4 Jobs to be done

People do not buy products, they hire them to make progress. The job has three
layers, and technical sellers usually address only the first:

| layer | question | gym example |
|---|---|---|
| **functional** | what task must get done? | answer enquiries and book trials without delay |
| **emotional** | how do they want to feel? | not guilty about ignoring messages at night |
| **social** | how do they want to be seen? | as a modern gym that responds instantly |

The emotional and social layers determine price and messaging. Two products with
identical features can differ threefold in price because one addresses the
owner's guilt and the other only answers messages. When your offer sounds
interchangeable, it is usually because you described only the functional job.

### 2.5 Positioning is a choice about the alternative

Positioning, following Ries and Trout, is the place you occupy in the buyer's
mind relative to alternatives. Two consequences are often missed:

**The alternative is usually not a competitor.** For a gym enquiry bot, the
alternatives are: do nothing, keep using WhatsApp manually, hire a receptionist,
ask a nephew, or buy a generic chatbot. You are not competing with other bot
vendors; you are competing with "hire a person" and "do nothing".

**Positioning is subtractive.** You choose what you are *not*. "I build websites,
apps, bots and automations" positions you as nothing. "I remove missed enquiries
for single-location gyms" positions you as one specific fix, and makes the price
conversation possible.

The Moore template (from *Crossing the Chasm*) forces every choice into one
sentence:

```
For   [target customer]
who   [statement of need or opportunity],
the   [product name]
is a  [product category]
that  [key benefit].
Unlike [primary competitive alternative],
our product [primary differentiation].
```

Filled in honestly:

```
For   single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities
who   lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm,
the   Enquiry Desk
is a  WhatsApp booking assistant
that  replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee.
Unlike hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month,
it costs 2,000 a month, never sleeps and never takes leave.
```

Every claim in that statement is checkable, and the alternative is a salary, not
a competitor. That is what makes it a position rather than a slogan.

**The stranger test:** read your statement to someone outside your field and ask
them to repeat it back. If they cannot, it is not positioned; it is described.

### 2.6 Entering a category versus creating one

| route | when it works | cost | risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **enter an existing category** | buyers already search for the term | low | price comparison, incumbents |
| **create a new category** | the problem is real but unnamed | high | you must teach the market first |

Entering is almost always correct for a solo seller. If people already search
"gym management software", be a gym management system with one sharp difference.
Category creation is a funded-startup game, because someone has to pay for the
education of an entire market.

The exception: when the existing category's price is anchored so low that your
economics break. Then you rename the problem, not the product.

## Examples

### Example 1: Ten interviews, coded for evidence

A developer interviews ten Patna gym owners about after-hours enquiries. The raw
notes look encouraging. Coding them by tier changes the conclusion.

| # | what they said | tier | what it actually licenses |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Great idea, send details" | 0 | nothing |
| 2 | "Yes we lose messages at night" | 1 | nothing |
| 3 | "Last month we lost a wedding group of 12 because we replied Monday" | 2 | sharpen the question |
| 4 | "We hired a night receptionist in June, 14k a month" | 3 | proof of spend |
| 5 | "I check messages myself at 11pm, it's killing me" | 2 | emotional layer |
| 6 | "We tried a chatbot, it was useless, cancelled in a month" | 3 | a real objection to design around |
| 7 | "Send me a proposal" | 1 | nothing |
| 8 | "Owner's son handles it, talk to him" | 2 | buying process |
| 9 | "Can you do it for 5,000?" | 2 | price anchor, low |
| 10 | "Paid 30,000 to an agency last year for this" | 5 | the ceiling is real |

Reading: **two tier-5/4 signals** (existing spend of ₹14k/month, ₹30k past
payment) and **three tier-3 signals**. Six of ten are noise. That ratio is
normal, and it is why ten interviews, not two, is the minimum.

The actionable conclusions:

- The price ceiling is around ₹30,000 setup and ₹2,000–3,000 monthly, because
  that is what this market has already paid.
- Objection 6 must be answered in the pitch, not discovered on the call: the
  previous chatbot failed. Differentiation is "books and collects payment", not
  "answers questions".
- The buyer is often not the owner but a family member; ask early.

### Example 2: Turning interview notes into a positioning statement

```python
"""Build and sanity-check positioning statements from coded interview data.

The check is not clever: it flags statements that describe a feature instead of
a person, or that name a competitor instead of the real alternative.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Positioning:
    who: str
    need: str
    name: str
    category: str
    benefit: str
    alternative: str
    difference: str

    def render(self) -> str:
        return (
            f"For {self.who} who {self.need}, {self.name} is a {self.category} "
            f"that {self.benefit}. Unlike {self.alternative}, {self.difference}."
        )

    def problems(self) -> list[str]:
        out = []
        if any(w in self.who.lower() for w in ("everyone", "businesses", "people", "smes")):
            out.append("'who' is a demographic, not a situation with a trigger")
        if any(w in self.alternative.lower() for w in ("competitor", "other vendors", "no one")):
            out.append("the alternative should be what they do today, including 'nothing'")
        if not any(ch.isdigit() for ch in self.difference):
            out.append("no number in the differentiator: nothing to verify")
        return out


p = Positioning(
    who="single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities",
    need="lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm",
    name="Enquiry Desk",
    category="WhatsApp booking assistant",
    benefit="replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee",
    alternative="hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month",
    difference="it costs 2,000 a month and never takes leave",
)

print(p.render())
print("problems:", p.problems() or "none")
```

Expected output:

```
For single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities who lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm, Enquiry Desk is a WhatsApp booking assistant that replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee. Unlike hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month, it costs 2,000 a month and never takes leave.
problems: none
```

Change `who` to "SMBs" and `difference` to "it is better and faster" and the
function reports two problems. The value is not the linter; it is that the
sentence has to be finished before it can be checked.

## Summary

- An ICP is defined by situation, trigger, current workaround and cost of the problem; the trigger is what makes outreach timely.
- Problem interviews must ask about past behaviour and specifics, because people are kind and will compliment ideas they would never buy.
- Evidence has five usable tiers and only tier 3 and above justifies building; compliments and intentions do not.
- A job has functional, emotional and social layers, and the last two determine price and messaging.
- The real competitive alternative is usually a workaround, a salary or doing nothing, not another vendor.
- Positioning is subtractive and must survive the stranger test: a listener repeats it back correctly.
- Entering an existing category is cheaper than creating one, and category creation is a funded game.
