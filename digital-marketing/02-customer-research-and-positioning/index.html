Chapter 2 — Customer Research and Positioning

Learning Objectives​

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Write an ideal customer profile (ICP) that names a trigger event, not just a job title
  • Run a problem interview that produces evidence instead of compliments
  • Rank evidence into five tiers from opinion to payment, and refuse to build on the bottom two
  • Map the job the customer is trying to do, including its functional, emotional and social parts
  • Write a positioning statement using the Moore template and test it on a stranger
  • Decide between entering an existing category and creating a new one

Theory​

2.1 The ICP is a trigger, not a demographic​

An ideal customer profile is often written as "SMB owners, 25–45, India". That describes nobody in particular and cannot be acted on, because every decision after it is a guess.

A usable ICP has four parts:

partexamplewhy it matters
situationruns a single-location gym with 200–800 membersdefines the context of use
triggerjust hired a second receptionist, or lost enquiries on WhatsApptells you when to contact them
current workaroundowner answers messages personally at 11pmthe real competitor
cost of the problemroughly 20–40 missed enquiries a monththe ceiling on your price

The trigger is the part almost everyone omits, and it is the most valuable. A trigger converts a permanent maybe into a dated yes. "Gyms" is a market. "Gyms that just crossed 400 members and are losing enquiries at night" is a list you can build this afternoon.

The cost of the problem sets your price ceiling before you have written a single line of the offer. If missed enquiries cost ₹8,000 a month, a ₹25,000 setup plus ₹2,000 a month is defensible. If they cost ₹1,500 a month, it is not, and no amount of clever copy changes that.

2.2 Interviews that produce evidence​

Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test reduces to three rules:

  1. Ask about their life, not your idea. "How do you handle enquiries after closing time?" not "Would you use an AI bot?"
  2. Ask about the past, not the future. What they did last month is data. What they say they would do is a politeness.
  3. Ask for specifics, not opinions. "Which enquiry did you lose most recently?" not "Is this a big problem for you?"

The reason is that people are kind. They will tell you your idea is good to end the conversation. A question about the past is much harder to answer kindly and much easier to answer truthfully.

A 15-minute interview that works:

1. "Walk me through what happens when a customer messages you at 9pm."
2. "What did you do the last time that happened?"        <- past, specific
3. "How many times did that happen last week?"
4. "What did it cost you, roughly?"
5. "What have you already tried to fix it?"
6. "Who else is involved in deciding something like this?"

Question 5 is the one that surfaces competitors you did not know about, including "my nephew made a spreadsheet" and "nothing, we just lose them". Question 6 prevents the most common wasted month: selling to someone who cannot sign.

What you are listening for: a number, a name, a date, or an emotional spike. Anything else is noise. "Yeah, that's annoying" is noise. "We lost the Mehta wedding booking because nobody replied until Monday" is evidence.

2.3 The evidence hierarchy​

Not all research is equal. Weight it deliberately.

tierevidenceweightexample
5they paid moneydecisivea signed contract, an invoice
4they committed something costlystronga deposit, a calendar slot, an intro to their boss
3they did it themselvesstrongbuilt a spreadsheet, hired a person, wrote a process
2they described past behaviour in detailmoderatea specific incident with names and numbers
1they said they wouldweak"sounds useful, send me info"
0they complimented youworthless"great idea"

The rule: build on tier 3 and above. Tier 1 and 2 tell you where to look next; they do not justify writing code. This single rule prevents the most expensive mistake available to a technical founder, which is building a working product for a polite audience.

2.4 Jobs to be done​

People do not buy products, they hire them to make progress. The job has three layers, and technical sellers usually address only the first:

layerquestiongym example
functionalwhat task must get done?answer enquiries and book trials without delay
emotionalhow do they want to feel?not guilty about ignoring messages at night
socialhow do they want to be seen?as a modern gym that responds instantly

The emotional and social layers determine price and messaging. Two products with identical features can differ threefold in price because one addresses the owner's guilt and the other only answers messages. When your offer sounds interchangeable, it is usually because you described only the functional job.

2.5 Positioning is a choice about the alternative​

Positioning, following Ries and Trout, is the place you occupy in the buyer's mind relative to alternatives. Two consequences are often missed:

The alternative is usually not a competitor. For a gym enquiry bot, the alternatives are: do nothing, keep using WhatsApp manually, hire a receptionist, ask a nephew, or buy a generic chatbot. You are not competing with other bot vendors; you are competing with "hire a person" and "do nothing".

Positioning is subtractive. You choose what you are not. "I build websites, apps, bots and automations" positions you as nothing. "I remove missed enquiries for single-location gyms" positions you as one specific fix, and makes the price conversation possible.

The Moore template (from Crossing the Chasm) forces every choice into one sentence:

For   [target customer]
who   [statement of need or opportunity],
the   [product name]
is a  [product category]
that  [key benefit].
Unlike [primary competitive alternative],
our product [primary differentiation].

Filled in honestly:

For   single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities
who   lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm,
the   Enquiry Desk
is a  WhatsApp booking assistant
that  replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee.
Unlike hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month,
it costs 2,000 a month, never sleeps and never takes leave.

Every claim in that statement is checkable, and the alternative is a salary, not a competitor. That is what makes it a position rather than a slogan.

The stranger test: read your statement to someone outside your field and ask them to repeat it back. If they cannot, it is not positioned; it is described.

2.6 Entering a category versus creating one​

routewhen it workscostrisk
enter an existing categorybuyers already search for the termlowprice comparison, incumbents
create a new categorythe problem is real but unnamedhighyou must teach the market first

Entering is almost always correct for a solo seller. If people already search "gym management software", be a gym management system with one sharp difference. Category creation is a funded-startup game, because someone has to pay for the education of an entire market.

The exception: when the existing category's price is anchored so low that your economics break. Then you rename the problem, not the product.

Examples​

Example 1: Ten interviews, coded for evidence​

A developer interviews ten Patna gym owners about after-hours enquiries. The raw notes look encouraging. Coding them by tier changes the conclusion.

#what they saidtierwhat it actually licenses
1"Great idea, send details"0nothing
2"Yes we lose messages at night"1nothing
3"Last month we lost a wedding group of 12 because we replied Monday"2sharpen the question
4"We hired a night receptionist in June, 14k a month"3proof of spend
5"I check messages myself at 11pm, it's killing me"2emotional layer
6"We tried a chatbot, it was useless, cancelled in a month"3a real objection to design around
7"Send me a proposal"1nothing
8"Owner's son handles it, talk to him"2buying process
9"Can you do it for 5,000?"2price anchor, low
10"Paid 30,000 to an agency last year for this"5the ceiling is real

Reading: two tier-5/4 signals (existing spend of ₹14k/month, ₹30k past payment) and three tier-3 signals. Six of ten are noise. That ratio is normal, and it is why ten interviews, not two, is the minimum.

The actionable conclusions:

  • The price ceiling is around ₹30,000 setup and ₹2,000–3,000 monthly, because that is what this market has already paid.
  • Objection 6 must be answered in the pitch, not discovered on the call: the previous chatbot failed. Differentiation is "books and collects payment", not "answers questions".
  • The buyer is often not the owner but a family member; ask early.

Example 2: Turning interview notes into a positioning statement​

"""Build and sanity-check positioning statements from coded interview data.

The check is not clever: it flags statements that describe a feature instead of
a person, or that name a competitor instead of the real alternative.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Positioning:
    who: str
    need: str
    name: str
    category: str
    benefit: str
    alternative: str
    difference: str

    def render(self) -> str:
        return (
            f"For {self.who} who {self.need}, {self.name} is a {self.category} "
            f"that {self.benefit}. Unlike {self.alternative}, {self.difference}."
        )

    def problems(self) -> list[str]:
        out = []
        if any(w in self.who.lower() for w in ("everyone", "businesses", "people", "smes")):
            out.append("'who' is a demographic, not a situation with a trigger")
        if any(w in self.alternative.lower() for w in ("competitor", "other vendors", "no one")):
            out.append("the alternative should be what they do today, including 'nothing'")
        if not any(ch.isdigit() for ch in self.difference):
            out.append("no number in the differentiator: nothing to verify")
        return out


p = Positioning(
    who="single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities",
    need="lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm",
    name="Enquiry Desk",
    category="WhatsApp booking assistant",
    benefit="replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee",
    alternative="hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month",
    difference="it costs 2,000 a month and never takes leave",
)

print(p.render())
print("problems:", p.problems() or "none")

Expected output:

For single-location gyms in tier-2 Indian cities who lose enquiries because nobody answers WhatsApp after 8pm, Enquiry Desk is a WhatsApp booking assistant that replies in seconds, books trials and collects the fee. Unlike hiring a second receptionist at 14,000 a month, it costs 2,000 a month and never takes leave.
problems: none

Change who to "SMBs" and difference to "it is better and faster" and the function reports two problems. The value is not the linter; it is that the sentence has to be finished before it can be checked.

Summary​

  • An ICP is defined by situation, trigger, current workaround and cost of the problem; the trigger is what makes outreach timely.
  • Problem interviews must ask about past behaviour and specifics, because people are kind and will compliment ideas they would never buy.
  • Evidence has five usable tiers and only tier 3 and above justifies building; compliments and intentions do not.
  • A job has functional, emotional and social layers, and the last two determine price and messaging.
  • The real competitive alternative is usually a workaround, a salary or doing nothing, not another vendor.
  • Positioning is subtractive and must survive the stranger test: a listener repeats it back correctly.
  • Entering an existing category is cheaper than creating one, and category creation is a funded game.

Exercises​

Review Questions​

  1. Why does a trigger event matter more than a job title when building a prospect list?
  2. State the three rules of a problem interview and give a question that violates each.
  3. Which evidence tier is "I would definitely pay for that", and why is it not enough to start building?
  4. Explain the difference between the functional and social layers of a job, using a product you own.
  5. Why is "unlike other agencies" a positioning failure?

Application Problems​

  1. Write an ICP for your offer with all four parts. Mark the trigger with the source you would use to find people who just experienced it.
  2. Run five problem interviews of at least ten minutes. Code each answer into a tier. Report your tier-5 and tier-3 count, and state whether it justifies building.
  3. Write two positioning statements with the Moore template: one entering an existing category, one creating a new one. Give the stranger test to two people and record how they repeated it back.

Challenge Problem​

You have ten interviews and six are tier 0 or 1. Design the next three interviews so that each one can only produce tier 3 evidence or better, without asking a leading question. Then state the decision rule you will apply if all three produce tier 1, and what you will build instead.
