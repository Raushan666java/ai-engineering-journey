Chapter 11 — Launch and Go-to-Market

Learning Objectives​

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Decide whether your offer needs a launch at all, and say why most services do not
  • Run a pre-sale that tests demand before you build the thing
  • Sequence a fourteen-day launch with dated actions and numeric targets
  • Price a founding cohort without devaluing the offer permanently
  • Compute how many buyers a revenue target actually requires
  • Diagnose the three ways launches fail and which one you are at risk of

Theory​

11.1 Most offers should not launch​

A launch is a coordinated window in which attention is concentrated on one offer. It works when there is a list or an audience to concentrate.

For a service business — contract work, consulting, a productised retainer — there is no launch. There is a steady pipeline, and the work is the channel programme from chapters 5 to 8. Announcing "I am now available" to nobody is not a launch; it is a post.

offer typelaunch?because
contract / consultingnorevenue is continuous, and demand is not synchronised
productised servicesometimesa founding-client offer can create urgency
course or cohortyesstart dates create real deadlines
software productmaybeonly if you already have an audience
anything with no listnoa launch to an empty room is a diary entry

The rule: a launch requires an audience and a deadline. Without both, spend the effort on the channel programme instead.

11.2 Pre-sale: the only honest launch test​

A pre-sale is selling the thing before it exists. It is uncomfortable and it is the cheapest possible validation, because the alternative is building first and discovering the answer later at full cost.

The mechanics:

1  Describe the offer precisely: outcome, contents, dates, price.
2  Set a real deadline and a real capacity limit.
3  Ask for money, not for interest. A deposit or full payment.
4  If the threshold is not met, refund and do not build.

The threshold should be decided in advance: "if ten people pay, I build it; if three do, I refund and ask them what they actually wanted."

Refusing to take money until it is built is the most common form of self-deception in course creation. Interest costs the buyer nothing and predicts almost nothing. Payment is the only signal with weight, which is exactly why it belongs in the evidence hierarchy from chapter 2 at the top.

11.3 The fourteen-day launch sequence​

For an offer with an existing list or a reachable community. Each row has an owner-action and a number.

dayactiontarget
−14announce the problem you are solving, no offerengagement baseline
−12publish the free diagnostic or checklist50 downloads
−10share one proof: a result, a teardown, a case20 replies or comments
−7open a waitlist with the price shown30 waitlist signups
−5answer the top three objections publiclyreduce replies asking "how much"
−3announce the deadline and the capacity10 pre-orders
−1last call, with the exact closing time15 pre-orders

+1deliver, or begin deliveryfirst session within 7 days
+7collect one testimonial and one case study1 of each

Notice that only two of the ten rows sell. The other eight exist to make the two selling rows credible, which is why a launch that begins on day −1 usually fails.

11.4 Pricing a founding cohort​

A founding price is a real discount in exchange for something real: feedback, a testimonial, and tolerance for rough edges. That trade must be stated, or the discount permanently anchors the price.

elementrule
the discount30–50% off the intended price, time-boxed to the cohort
what the buyer givesfeedback sessions, a testimonial, permission to use results
when it endsa named date, and then the price rises for everyone
the next pricepublished at the same time, so the discount has a reference

The failure mode: a permanent "launch price" that never rises. Buyers learn the price is fictional, and the discount teaches them to wait for the next one.

11.5 How many buyers a target needs​

Revenue targets are usually stated as money and rarely converted into a count. The conversion makes the plan testable.

revenue target       = 2,00,000
price                = 4,999
buyers required      = 41
list conversion      = 2-5% for a warm list at this price, warmer end if the
                       offer is specific
list size required   = 41 / 0.03 = about 1,370 warm subscribers

If the list is 200 people, the target requires a 20% conversion, which is not a plan; it is a hope. The honest responses, in order: raise the price and lower the count, extend the timeline to build the list, or change the offer to something that does not need a list.

That single calculation has prevented more failed course launches than any amount of copywriting advice.

11.6 The three ways launches fail​

failuresymptomcause
launching to silence40 impressions, no repliesno audience, or an audience built on the wrong topic
launching to interestmany "sounds great", zero paymentsasked for opinions instead of money
launching too latethe thing is finished before anyone is askedbuilding felt safer than selling

The third is the most common among technical builders, and the most expensive, because it converts months of work into a sunk cost before the first signal arrives.

11.7 After the launch​

A launch is a spike; a business is the line underneath it. Three things decide whether the spike becomes a line:

  • retention: did buyers get the promised outcome, and can you prove it
  • proof: one testimonial and one case study, collected within a week
  • repeatability: a channel that produces the next cohort without a launch

If the next cohort can only be sold by launching again to the same list, the business is a series of spikes with decay between them.

Examples​

Example 1: Pre-sale arithmetic before writing a single lesson​

"""Decide whether to build a course, using the pre-sale threshold.

The point is not the model. The point is that the threshold is chosen before
anyone is emotionally invested in the answer.
"""
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class PreSale:
    price: int
    threshold_buyers: int
    reachable_list: int
    expected_conversion: float   # judgement, 2-5% for a warm specific offer
    build_hours: int

    def expected_buyers(self) -> float:
        return self.reachable_list * self.expected_conversion

    def verdict(self) -> str:
        buyers = self.expected_buyers()
        if buyers >= self.threshold_buyers:
            return "build: threshold reached on paper, confirm with real payments"
        shortfall = self.threshold_buyers - buyers
        return (f"do not build yet: short by {shortfall:.0f} buyers; "
                f"need about {self.threshold_buyers / self.expected_conversion:,.0f} "
                f"list subscribers at this conversion")

    def hourly_value_if_sold(self) -> float:
        return (self.threshold_buyers * self.price) / self.build_hours


small = PreSale(price=4_999, threshold_buyers=10, reachable_list=200,
                expected_conversion=0.03, build_hours=120)
print(round(small.expected_buyers(), 1), "expected buyers")
print(small.verdict())
print(f"rs{small.hourly_value_if_sold():,.0f} per build hour if the threshold is just met")

larger = PreSale(price=4_999, threshold_buyers=10, reachable_list=1_500,
                 expected_conversion=0.03, build_hours=120)
print("\n", round(larger.expected_buyers(), 1), "expected buyers")
print(larger.verdict())
print(f"rs{larger.hourly_value_if_sold():,.0f} per build hour if the threshold is just met")

Expected output:

6.0 expected buyers
do not build yet: short by 4 buyers; need about 333 list subscribers at this conversion
rs417 per build hour if the threshold is just met

 45.0 expected buyers
build: threshold reached on paper, confirm with real payments
rs417 per build hour if the threshold is just met

Two lessons, both uncomfortable. With 200 subscribers the course does not clear its own threshold, so the correct action is to build the list rather than the course. And even in the healthy case, the threshold scenario pays ₹417 per build hour, which is less than a single hour of contract work at a normal rate.

That is not an argument against courses. It is the argument for treating the first course as marketing spend that happens to be paid, and for pricing later cohorts higher once proof exists.

Example 2: A launch scorecard​

# Launch scorecard — cohort 1

Offer      "Laravel to AI engineer", 6 weeks, 12 lessons, 1 project
Price      rs4,999 founding, rs7,999 from cohort 2
Capacity   20
Deadline   day 0, 21:00 IST

Metric              Target    Actual    Notes
--------------------  -------  --------  ----------------------------
waitlist signups         30
pre-orders               10
revenue            rs49,990
refund requests            0
completion rate         70%
testimonials               3
next-cohort waitlist      15

Decision rule
  >= 10 paid             run cohort 1, publish the case study
  5-9 paid               run a reduced cohort, interview every buyer
  < 5 paid               refund, do not build, ask buyers what they wanted

The scorecard is written before the launch, including the decision rule. That ordering is the whole point: a rule invented after seeing the numbers is not a rule, it is a rationalisation.

Summary​

  • A launch needs both an audience and a deadline; services have neither and should run a pipeline instead.
  • Pre-selling is the only honest test of demand, and payment is the only signal with weight.
  • Only two of the ten days in a launch sequence sell; the rest exist to make those two credible.
  • A founding discount must buy something real and must end on a named date with a published next price.
  • Converting a revenue target into a required list size is what makes a launch plan testable.
  • Launches fail by silence, by interest without payment, or by building before asking.
  • A launch is a spike; retention, proof and a repeatable channel are what make a business.

Exercises​

Review Questions​

  1. Why does a consulting business not need a launch?
  2. What makes a pre-sale a test rather than a survey?
  3. Why must the decision rule be written before the launch?
  4. What does a founding discount buy, and what happens if it never ends?
  5. A target requires a 20% conversion from a 200-person list. What are the three honest responses?

Application Problems​

  1. Decide whether your offer needs a launch, and justify it against the table in 11.1.
  2. Design a pre-sale with a threshold, a deadline, a capacity and a refund rule. State what you would do if you got exactly half the threshold.
  3. Compute the list size your revenue target requires, then compare it with your actual reachable audience.

Challenge Problem​

You have 120 subscribers, no product yet, and a ₹1,00,000 revenue target in 60 days. Build a plan that reaches the target without launching to an audience you do not have, using at most two channels and no paid advertising. State the arithmetic at every step and the point at which you would abandon the target rather than the offer.
