# Chapter 1 — Marketing Foundations

## Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

- Define marketing as an exchange of value and distinguish it from advertising
- Explain why most marketing failures are positioning or distribution failures, not creative failures
- Segment a market and choose one segment using five testable criteria
- Size a market three ways (TAM, SAM, SOM) and state the assumptions behind each number
- Describe the 4Ps and the 7Ps and say when the extra three apply
- Draw the six-stage customer system and identify which stage your business is actually stuck in

## Theory

### 1.1 Marketing is an exchange, not a broadcast

The American Marketing Association defines marketing as "the activity, set of
institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and
exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and
society at large."

The operative word is **exchange**. Marketing succeeds when two parties each give
up something they value less in return for something they value more. A developer
gives time and skill; a client gives money. Both must believe they gain.

This definition immediately rules out the most common beginner behaviour:
broadcasting. Posting "I am available for freelance work" is not marketing,
because it proposes no specific exchange. It announces a supply with no stated
value, to an audience with no stated problem.

Three questions make an exchange concrete:

| question | what it forces |
|---|---|
| Who gives up what? | names the cost to each side, including switching cost |
| What is the alternative? | often "do nothing" or "keep the current vendor" |
| Why is this better than the alternative? | this is your entire value proposition |

If you cannot answer the third in one sentence, no amount of advertising will
help, because there is nothing to advertise.

### 1.2 The three ways marketing fails

Almost every failed marketing effort is one of these, in this order of frequency:

**No demand.** People do not have the problem, or do not have it badly enough to
pay. A perfectly executed campaign for something nobody wants converts at zero.
This is a research failure, fixed in chapter 2, and it cannot be fixed later.

**No differentiation.** People have the problem and are already solving it. Your
offer looks identical to three alternatives, so the buyer defaults to the
cheapest or the one they already know. This is a positioning failure, fixed in
chapter 2, and priced in chapter 3.

**No distribution.** People want it, your offer is distinct, and nobody ever
sees it. This is the failure that feels like a marketing failure but is really a
volume problem, fixed in chapters 5 to 8.

Notice what is missing: creative quality. Design and copywriting amplify a
working offer. They cannot rescue a broken one. This ordering is why the course
spends three chapters before it mentions a channel.

### 1.3 Segmentation: you cannot market to "everyone"

A market is a set of people with a need and the ability to pay. It is always
heterogeneous: a gym owner in Patna and a hospital chain in Hyderabad may both
want a WhatsApp bot, and they will buy it for different reasons, at different
prices, through different people.

Segmentation splits the market into groups that respond similarly. A useful
segment passes five tests, each of which can be checked rather than assumed:

| criterion | the question | how to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Measurable** | can you count them? | a directory, a trade body, a platform's company filter |
| **Accessible** | can you reach them at a cost you can afford? | is there a list, a community, an event, a search term |
| **Substantial** | is it big enough to matter? | count × realistic price |
| **Differentiable** | do they respond differently from other segments? | two segments, two messages, different reply rates |
| **Actionable** | can you build a programme for them specifically? | you can name the first ten by hand |

The last test is the one beginners skip. If you cannot write down ten specific
organisations or people in the segment, it is not a segment, it is a demographic.

### 1.4 Market sizing without self-deception

Three numbers, each a strict subset of the previous:

- **TAM** (total addressable market): everyone who could conceivably buy this
  category, at your price.
- **SAM** (serviceable addressable market): the part you can actually serve given
  your language, geography, channel and capability.
- **SOM** (serviceable obtainable market): the part you can realistically win in
  a stated period with the resources you have.

The only number that matters for a plan is SOM, and the only thing that makes
SOM credible is that it is derived from a channel capacity rather than a
percentage.

A worked comparison, both figures being **estimates** with assumptions stated:

```
Bad:  "India has 1.4 million software developers. If I get 1%, that is 14,000
       customers." -> says nothing about how anyone would ever reach them.

Good: "I can send 20 personalised emails a day, 5 days a week, so 400 a month.
       Historical reply rate for this message shape is 10-20%, so 40-80 replies.
       A third of replies take a call: about 13-27 calls. One in four calls signs
       a small contract: 3-7 clients a month, at 3 months average engagement."
```

The second version is longer, uglier, and it is the only one you can plan against.
It also fails honestly: if the reply rate is 2%, the business is not viable at
20 emails a day, and you learn that in week two instead of month six.

### 1.5 The 4Ps and the 7Ps

McCarthy's four Ps describe the controllable variables:

| P | what it decides |
|---|---|
| **Product** | what is delivered, including scope boundaries |
| **Price** | what is exchanged, including terms and payment schedule |
| **Place** | where the exchange happens: online, on-site, marketplace, direct |
| **Promotion** | how the buyer learns, and is reminded |

Booms and Bitner added three for services, because a service cannot be inspected
before purchase:

| P | what it decides |
|---|---|
| **People** | who delivers it; for a solo developer, this is you |
| **Process** | how delivery runs: discovery, milestones, handover |
| **Physical evidence** | what the buyer can see: proposal, repo, case study, invoice |

For contract work, **physical evidence** is doing most of the selling, and it is
the thing technical sellers most often omit. A runnable repository is physical
evidence. A claim of three years' experience is not.

### 1.6 The six-stage customer system

Marketing is a system with a leak at exactly one place at a time. Diagnose
before treating.

```
attention -> interest -> trust -> exchange -> retention -> referral
```

| stage | the question | the metric | typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| attention | do they see you? | impressions, reach | nobody knows you exist |
| interest | do they stop? | click rate, reply rate | the headline is about you, not them |
| trust | do they believe you? | time on page, call booked | claims with no proof |
| exchange | do they pay? | conversion rate | no clear price or next step |
| retention | do they come back? | repeat rate, churn | delivery, not marketing |
| referral | do they tell others? | referral share | nobody asks |

The diagnostic rule: **find the first stage whose rate is far below the next
one.** A thousand impressions and two clicks is an interest problem. Two hundred
clicks and no calls is a trust problem. Ten calls and no signatures is a pricing
or process problem.

Treating the wrong stage is the most expensive mistake in this course, because
each stage has its own cure and they are not interchangeable.

## Examples

### Example 1: Sizing a contract offer honestly

Offer: a WhatsApp enquiry-and-booking bot for gyms, clinics and coaching centres.
Price: ₹25,000 setup plus ₹2,000 a month. All figures below are **estimates**,
except the channel capacity, which is a physical limit.

```
Step 1 - define the segment
  Independent gyms and clinics in Patna with a phone number and a Google listing.
  Not chains. Not hospitals. Not Delhi.

Step 2 - count it (measurable)
  A Google Maps scrape for "gym" within Patna returns roughly 300-400 listings
  with a phone number. Say 350.

Step 3 - check the price against their economics (differentiable)
  A receptionist answering enquiries costs about 12,000-18,000 a month.
  A 25,000 setup plus 2,000 a month is defensible if it removes even part of
  that cost. If it does not, the segment cannot afford the offer and the offer
  is wrong, not the segment.

Step 4 - compute SOM from capacity, not from a percentage
  Walk-ins possible: 4 per day, 5 days a week = 80 a month, but the same 350
  businesses are revisited, so realistically 25 new conversations a month.
  A face-to-face conversation converts to a paid pilot at roughly 1 in 10 for
  local service software. So 2-3 pilots a month.

Step 5 - check it is substantial
  2.5 pilots x 25,000 = 62,500 in setup, plus 2.5 x 2,000 = 5,000 a month
  recurring, growing as pilots convert. Small. Viable only if delivery takes
  under 15 hours per pilot.
```

Conclusion: the segment is real and reachable, the SOM is 2–3 clients a month,
and the binding constraint is delivery time rather than demand. That is a
sentence you can act on. "1% of 1.4 million developers" is not.

### Example 2: Scoring candidate segments instead of arguing about them

The five criteria are only useful if they are applied consistently. This script
turns the comparison into arithmetic, so a choice can be defended.

```python
"""Score candidate segments on the five criteria. Weights are a judgement
call; the value is that the judgement is written down and can be changed."""
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Segment:
    name: str
    measurable: int      # 1-5: can you count them from a real source?
    accessible: int      # 1-5: is there a list, community or search term?
    substantial: int     # 1-5: count x price is worth the effort?
    differentiable: int  # 1-5: do they need a different message?
    actionable: int      # 1-5: can you name ten of them today?
    price: int           # realistic first-project price in rupees


WEIGHTS = {                # sum to 1.0
    "measurable": 0.15,
    "accessible": 0.30,    # heaviest: reachability is the usual bottleneck
    "substantial": 0.20,
    "differentiable": 0.15,
    "actionable": 0.20,
}


def score(seg: Segment) -> float:
    total = sum(getattr(seg, k) * w for k, w in WEIGHTS.items())
    return round(total / 5 * 100, 1)


candidates = [
    Segment("Patna gyms", 4, 5, 2, 4, 5, 25_000),
    Segment("Indian D2C brands", 3, 3, 4, 4, 2, 150_000),
    Segment("US seed startups", 3, 2, 5, 5, 1, 400_000),
    Segment("Indian dev agencies", 5, 5, 3, 3, 5, 60_000),
]

for s in sorted(candidates, key=score, reverse=True):
    print(f"{score(s):>5}  {s.name:<24} rs{s.price:>7,}")
```

Expected output:

```
 86.0  Indian dev agencies      rs 60,000
 82.0  Patna gyms               rs 25,000
 63.0  Indian D2C brands        rs150,000
 60.0  US seed startups         rs400,000
```

What the example demonstrates: the two highest-priced segments rank last, because
accessibility dominates at the start and neither can name ten buyers today. Dev
agencies edge out Patna gyms despite a lower price, because they score 5 on both
measurability and actionability — a directory of agencies is a list you can open
this afternoon.

That is not a universal law, it is a consequence of the weights, and the weights
are visible so they can be argued with. When you have proof of demand, raise
`substantial` and the ranking changes. That is how a strategy evolves without
being rewritten.

## Summary

- Marketing is the exchange of value between two parties, not the broadcast of messages to an audience.
- Failures occur in a predictable order: no demand, then no differentiation, then no distribution; creative quality is never the first cause.
- A segment must be measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable and actionable, and the last one is the one beginners skip.
- TAM, SAM and SOM are nested, and only SOM is plannable, because only SOM can be derived from a physical channel limit.
- The 4Ps cover product, price, place and promotion; services add people, process and physical evidence, and evidence is what technical sellers omit.
- The customer system has six stages, and a correct diagnosis names the single stage where the rate collapses.
- Arithmetic with stated assumptions is the difference between a plan and a hope.
