Chapter 1 — Marketing Foundations

Learning Objectives​

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Define marketing as an exchange of value and distinguish it from advertising
  • Explain why most marketing failures are positioning or distribution failures, not creative failures
  • Segment a market and choose one segment using five testable criteria
  • Size a market three ways (TAM, SAM, SOM) and state the assumptions behind each number
  • Describe the 4Ps and the 7Ps and say when the extra three apply
  • Draw the six-stage customer system and identify which stage your business is actually stuck in

Theory​

1.1 Marketing is an exchange, not a broadcast​

The American Marketing Association defines marketing as "the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large."

The operative word is exchange. Marketing succeeds when two parties each give up something they value less in return for something they value more. A developer gives time and skill; a client gives money. Both must believe they gain.

This definition immediately rules out the most common beginner behaviour: broadcasting. Posting "I am available for freelance work" is not marketing, because it proposes no specific exchange. It announces a supply with no stated value, to an audience with no stated problem.

Three questions make an exchange concrete:

questionwhat it forces
Who gives up what?names the cost to each side, including switching cost
What is the alternative?often "do nothing" or "keep the current vendor"
Why is this better than the alternative?this is your entire value proposition

If you cannot answer the third in one sentence, no amount of advertising will help, because there is nothing to advertise.

1.2 The three ways marketing fails​

Almost every failed marketing effort is one of these, in this order of frequency:

No demand. People do not have the problem, or do not have it badly enough to pay. A perfectly executed campaign for something nobody wants converts at zero. This is a research failure, fixed in chapter 2, and it cannot be fixed later.

No differentiation. People have the problem and are already solving it. Your offer looks identical to three alternatives, so the buyer defaults to the cheapest or the one they already know. This is a positioning failure, fixed in chapter 2, and priced in chapter 3.

No distribution. People want it, your offer is distinct, and nobody ever sees it. This is the failure that feels like a marketing failure but is really a volume problem, fixed in chapters 5 to 8.

Notice what is missing: creative quality. Design and copywriting amplify a working offer. They cannot rescue a broken one. This ordering is why the course spends three chapters before it mentions a channel.

1.3 Segmentation: you cannot market to "everyone"​

A market is a set of people with a need and the ability to pay. It is always heterogeneous: a gym owner in Patna and a hospital chain in Hyderabad may both want a WhatsApp bot, and they will buy it for different reasons, at different prices, through different people.

Segmentation splits the market into groups that respond similarly. A useful segment passes five tests, each of which can be checked rather than assumed:

criterionthe questionhow to check
Measurablecan you count them?a directory, a trade body, a platform's company filter
Accessiblecan you reach them at a cost you can afford?is there a list, a community, an event, a search term
Substantialis it big enough to matter?count × realistic price
Differentiabledo they respond differently from other segments?two segments, two messages, different reply rates
Actionablecan you build a programme for them specifically?you can name the first ten by hand

The last test is the one beginners skip. If you cannot write down ten specific organisations or people in the segment, it is not a segment, it is a demographic.

1.4 Market sizing without self-deception​

Three numbers, each a strict subset of the previous:

  • TAM (total addressable market): everyone who could conceivably buy this category, at your price.
  • SAM (serviceable addressable market): the part you can actually serve given your language, geography, channel and capability.
  • SOM (serviceable obtainable market): the part you can realistically win in a stated period with the resources you have.

The only number that matters for a plan is SOM, and the only thing that makes SOM credible is that it is derived from a channel capacity rather than a percentage.

A worked comparison, both figures being estimates with assumptions stated:

Bad:  "India has 1.4 million software developers. If I get 1%, that is 14,000
       customers." -> says nothing about how anyone would ever reach them.

Good: "I can send 20 personalised emails a day, 5 days a week, so 400 a month.
       Historical reply rate for this message shape is 10-20%, so 40-80 replies.
       A third of replies take a call: about 13-27 calls. One in four calls signs
       a small contract: 3-7 clients a month, at 3 months average engagement."

The second version is longer, uglier, and it is the only one you can plan against. It also fails honestly: if the reply rate is 2%, the business is not viable at 20 emails a day, and you learn that in week two instead of month six.

1.5 The 4Ps and the 7Ps​

McCarthy's four Ps describe the controllable variables:

Pwhat it decides
Productwhat is delivered, including scope boundaries
Pricewhat is exchanged, including terms and payment schedule
Placewhere the exchange happens: online, on-site, marketplace, direct
Promotionhow the buyer learns, and is reminded

Booms and Bitner added three for services, because a service cannot be inspected before purchase:

Pwhat it decides
Peoplewho delivers it; for a solo developer, this is you
Processhow delivery runs: discovery, milestones, handover
Physical evidencewhat the buyer can see: proposal, repo, case study, invoice

For contract work, physical evidence is doing most of the selling, and it is the thing technical sellers most often omit. A runnable repository is physical evidence. A claim of three years' experience is not.

1.6 The six-stage customer system​

Marketing is a system with a leak at exactly one place at a time. Diagnose before treating.

attention -> interest -> trust -> exchange -> retention -> referral
stagethe questionthe metrictypical failure
attentiondo they see you?impressions, reachnobody knows you exist
interestdo they stop?click rate, reply ratethe headline is about you, not them
trustdo they believe you?time on page, call bookedclaims with no proof
exchangedo they pay?conversion rateno clear price or next step
retentiondo they come back?repeat rate, churndelivery, not marketing
referraldo they tell others?referral sharenobody asks

The diagnostic rule: find the first stage whose rate is far below the next one. A thousand impressions and two clicks is an interest problem. Two hundred clicks and no calls is a trust problem. Ten calls and no signatures is a pricing or process problem.

Treating the wrong stage is the most expensive mistake in this course, because each stage has its own cure and they are not interchangeable.

Examples​

Example 1: Sizing a contract offer honestly​

Offer: a WhatsApp enquiry-and-booking bot for gyms, clinics and coaching centres. Price: ₹25,000 setup plus ₹2,000 a month. All figures below are estimates, except the channel capacity, which is a physical limit.

Step 1 - define the segment
  Independent gyms and clinics in Patna with a phone number and a Google listing.
  Not chains. Not hospitals. Not Delhi.

Step 2 - count it (measurable)
  A Google Maps scrape for "gym" within Patna returns roughly 300-400 listings
  with a phone number. Say 350.

Step 3 - check the price against their economics (differentiable)
  A receptionist answering enquiries costs about 12,000-18,000 a month.
  A 25,000 setup plus 2,000 a month is defensible if it removes even part of
  that cost. If it does not, the segment cannot afford the offer and the offer
  is wrong, not the segment.

Step 4 - compute SOM from capacity, not from a percentage
  Walk-ins possible: 4 per day, 5 days a week = 80 a month, but the same 350
  businesses are revisited, so realistically 25 new conversations a month.
  A face-to-face conversation converts to a paid pilot at roughly 1 in 10 for
  local service software. So 2-3 pilots a month.

Step 5 - check it is substantial
  2.5 pilots x 25,000 = 62,500 in setup, plus 2.5 x 2,000 = 5,000 a month
  recurring, growing as pilots convert. Small. Viable only if delivery takes
  under 15 hours per pilot.

Conclusion: the segment is real and reachable, the SOM is 2–3 clients a month, and the binding constraint is delivery time rather than demand. That is a sentence you can act on. "1% of 1.4 million developers" is not.

Example 2: Scoring candidate segments instead of arguing about them​

The five criteria are only useful if they are applied consistently. This script turns the comparison into arithmetic, so a choice can be defended.

"""Score candidate segments on the five criteria. Weights are a judgement
call; the value is that the judgement is written down and can be changed."""
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Segment:
    name: str
    measurable: int      # 1-5: can you count them from a real source?
    accessible: int      # 1-5: is there a list, community or search term?
    substantial: int     # 1-5: count x price is worth the effort?
    differentiable: int  # 1-5: do they need a different message?
    actionable: int      # 1-5: can you name ten of them today?
    price: int           # realistic first-project price in rupees


WEIGHTS = {                # sum to 1.0
    "measurable": 0.15,
    "accessible": 0.30,    # heaviest: reachability is the usual bottleneck
    "substantial": 0.20,
    "differentiable": 0.15,
    "actionable": 0.20,
}


def score(seg: Segment) -> float:
    total = sum(getattr(seg, k) * w for k, w in WEIGHTS.items())
    return round(total / 5 * 100, 1)


candidates = [
    Segment("Patna gyms", 4, 5, 2, 4, 5, 25_000),
    Segment("Indian D2C brands", 3, 3, 4, 4, 2, 150_000),
    Segment("US seed startups", 3, 2, 5, 5, 1, 400_000),
    Segment("Indian dev agencies", 5, 5, 3, 3, 5, 60_000),
]

for s in sorted(candidates, key=score, reverse=True):
    print(f"{score(s):>5}  {s.name:<24} rs{s.price:>7,}")

Expected output:

 86.0  Indian dev agencies      rs 60,000
 82.0  Patna gyms               rs 25,000
 63.0  Indian D2C brands        rs150,000
 60.0  US seed startups         rs400,000

What the example demonstrates: the two highest-priced segments rank last, because accessibility dominates at the start and neither can name ten buyers today. Dev agencies edge out Patna gyms despite a lower price, because they score 5 on both measurability and actionability — a directory of agencies is a list you can open this afternoon.

That is not a universal law, it is a consequence of the weights, and the weights are visible so they can be argued with. When you have proof of demand, raise substantial and the ranking changes. That is how a strategy evolves without being rewritten.

Summary​

  • Marketing is the exchange of value between two parties, not the broadcast of messages to an audience.
  • Failures occur in a predictable order: no demand, then no differentiation, then no distribution; creative quality is never the first cause.
  • A segment must be measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable and actionable, and the last one is the one beginners skip.
  • TAM, SAM and SOM are nested, and only SOM is plannable, because only SOM can be derived from a physical channel limit.
  • The 4Ps cover product, price, place and promotion; services add people, process and physical evidence, and evidence is what technical sellers omit.
  • The customer system has six stages, and a correct diagnosis names the single stage where the rate collapses.
  • Arithmetic with stated assumptions is the difference between a plan and a hope.

Exercises​

Review Questions​

  1. Give an example of marketing activity that creates no exchange, and explain what is missing.
  2. Why is "1% of a large market" not a market sizing method? What replaces it?
  3. Which of the five segmentation criteria is most often satisfied on paper but false in practice, and why?
  4. A service business has 400 landing-page visits, 3 enquiries and 1 sale. Which stage of the customer system fails first, and what evidence would confirm it?
  5. Explain why physical evidence matters more for a solo consultant than for a product company.

Application Problems​

  1. Take your own offer and write the three exchange questions from section 1.1. For "what is the alternative", list at least three alternatives that are not competitors.
  2. Run the segment-scoring script on four segments you could actually serve. Change the weights once and explain which weight changed the winner and why that is defensible.
  3. Size your offer with a channel-capacity calculation like Example 1. State every assumption and mark which ones are measured versus estimated.

Challenge Problem​

A developer claims: "My market is 5 million small businesses in India, so even 0.01% is 500 clients." Construct the strongest possible defence of that claim, then dismantle it using only the five segmentation criteria and channel capacity. Conclude with the smallest segment that would still be a real business, and say how you would reach all of it in 30 days.
