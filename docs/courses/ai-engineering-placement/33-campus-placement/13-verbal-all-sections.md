## Section 1 — Grammar Rules (High-Frequency)

### Rule Box

```
Subject-verb agreement:
  Singular subjects take singular verbs (The manager IS going).
  "Each/Every/Neither/Either" → singular.
  "The committee/the team" → singular verb (collective noun acting as one unit).
Tenses: since + point in time, for + duration.
Prepositions: allergic TO, good AT, married TO, different FROM, rely ON, capable OF.
Countable vs uncountable: fewer (countable) vs less (uncountable); number vs amount.
Parallel structure: and/or must join the same forms.
```

### Key Insight

90% of grammar errors in aptitude tests are one of: subject-verb agreement, since/for, or preposition choice. Scan for the subject first, then the verb.

### Worked Examples

1. "The committee ___ yet to decide." → **has** (committee = singular unit).
2. "She is allergic ___ peanuts." → **to**.
3. "He has been working here ___ five years." → **for** (duration); "___ 2020" → **since**.
4. "Each of the boys ___ completed their homework." → **has** (each = singular); "their" should be "his" for boys.
5. "The manager with his team ___ going." → **is** (subject = manager, not team).

### Common Trap

Prepositional phrases between subject and verb ("manager with his team") trick you into matching the wrong noun. The subject is before the prepositional phrase.

## Section 2 — Vocabulary & Word Meaning

### Strategy Box

```
Root words: bene (good), mal (bad), spect (look), dict (say), ject (throw), cred (believe)
Prefixes: un-/im-/in-/ir- (not), pre- (before), post- (after), inter- (between), sub- (under)
Suffixes: -tion/-ment (noun), -ous/-ive (adjective), -ly (adverb)
If unknown: break into root + prefix, or eliminate options with clearly wrong tone.
```

### Key Insight

You don't need to know every word — you need to know the LIKELY meaning. Roots and prefixes cover ~60% of vocabulary questions. Tone matching (positive/negative) eliminates half the options.

### Worked Examples

1. "benefactor" → bene (good) + factor (maker) → one who does good → **donor**.
2. "malicious" → mal (bad) → **harmful intent**.
3. "introspective" → intro (within) + spect (look) → **looking inward**.
4. "postpone" → post (after) → **defer**.
5. "incredible" → in (not) + cred (believe) → **unbelievable**.

### Common Trap

Words with familiar parts but opposite meanings: "invaluable" = so valuable it can't be priced (NOT worthless). "flammable" = easily set on fire.

## Section 3 — Synonyms & Antonyms

### Method

1. Read the target word, define it in one phrase.
2. Synonym: pick the closest match, not the related-but-different word.
3. Antonym: pick the opposite; beware of near-synonyms disguised as answers.
4. When stuck: think of a sentence with the word, then replace options in the sentence.

### Key Insight

Test words in a sentence — context disambiguates. For antonyms, compare option pairs: if two options are synonyms of each other, neither can be the antonym.

### Worked Examples

1. Synonym of "eloquent" → **fluent**.
2. Antonym of "shallow" → **deep**.
3. Synonym of "abundant" → **plentiful**.
4. Antonym of "transparent" → **opaque**.
5. Synonym of "benevolent" → **kind**; antonym → **malevolent**.

### Common Trap

Words that share a domain but not the meaning: synonym of "quick" is "fast", not "hurry" (verb). Match parts of speech.

## Section 4 — Spelling

### Method

1. Read the word aloud mentally.
2. Check for common error zones: double letters (accommodate, embarrass), ie/ei (receive = ei after c, believe = ie), silent letters (definitely, separate), suffixes (preferred, occurring).
3. When two spellings look close, test the rule: "i before e except after c".

### Key Insight

The most misspelled words in placement tests: accommodate, definitely, separate, government, privilege, occurrence, maintenance, conscience.

### Worked Examples

1. Correct spelling? A) accomodate B) accommodate → **B**.
2. Correct spelling? A) seperate B) separate → **B**.
3. Correct spelling? A) definately B) definitely → **B**.
4. Correct spelling? A) receive B) recieve → **A** (ei after c).
5. Correct spelling? A) privelege B) privilege → **B**.

### Common Trap

"recieve" looks right but violates the rule. Watch "preferred" (double r before suffix) vs "prefered".

## Section 5 — Sentence Correction / Spot the Error

### Method

1. Read the whole sentence once for MEANING.
2. Identify the subject, check verb agreement.
3. Check tense consistency (same time frame = same tense).
4. Check parallel structure in lists.
5. Check pronoun agreement (singular/plural, gender, case).

### Key Insight

Error location is predictable: the error is usually subject-verb agreement, tense, or preposition. Every part of the sentence (A/B/C/D) is a suspect — check verbs first.

### Worked Examples

1. "He has been working in this company (A) since (B) five years (C)." → error: **since** → "for" (duration).
2. "Each of the boys have completed their homework." → **Each...has completed his homework**.
3. "The data shows that this year's sales are more better than last year's." → **...are better than...** ("more better" = double comparative).
4. "One of my friends are a doctor." → **One of my friends IS a doctor** (subject = one).
5. "Neither of the answers are correct." → **Neither of the answers IS correct**.

### Common Trap

"One of the X" — the subject is "one", singular. "More better", "most tallest", "very unique" are double-comparative errors.

## Section 6 — Fill in the Blanks

### Method

1. Read the FULL sentence to get the meaning direction (positive/negative).
2. Identify the word class needed (noun/verb/adjective/adverb, singular/plural).
3. Test each option in the sentence; pick the one that fits meaning AND grammar.
4. For two-blank questions: check each blank against its own half; the pair must both fit.

### Key Insight

Match the TONE. A negative context ("despite", "although", "however") needs a contrasting word. Also match the number (singular verb with singular noun).

### Worked Examples

1. "The committee ___ yet to decide." → **is**.
2. "She is allergic ___ peanuts." → **to**.
3. "If it ___ tomorrow, we will cancel the picnic." → **rains** (first conditional: if + present, will + base).
4. "He is ___ to succeed because he never gives up." → **bound** (bound to = certain to).
5. "The results were ___ than expected." → **better** (comparative needed).

### Common Trap

Conditional sentences: "If it rains" (present), NOT "if it will rain". Comparative forms need "than" context.

## Section 7 — Para Jumbles

### Method

1. Find the OPENER: the sentence that introduces the topic, usually with no pronoun reference backward ("A bank adopted an AI chatbot" — new info).
2. Find the CLOSER: the sentence that concludes ("Customer complaints dropped sharply" — result).
3. Link pronouns: "it", "they", "this company" must follow their referent.
4. Link time words: "first/then/after that/finally" sequence events.
5. Assemble, then read top-to-bottom for flow.

### Key Insight

The opener is almost never a sentence starting with "The company then...", "As a result...", "They...". Chronology markers ("then", "as a result") chain the middle.

### Worked Examples

1. (a) The company then trained staff on the new tool. (b) Customer complaints dropped sharply. (c) A bank adopted an AI chatbot. (d) As a result, response time halved. → **c, a, d, b** (adopt → train → result → final outcome).
2. (a) This keeps the soil fertile. (b) Farmers rotate crops every season. (c) Different crops use different nutrients. → **b, c, a**.
3. (a) She finally reached the office. (b) The bus was delayed by an hour. (c) She had left home on time. → **c, b, a**.
4. (a) The report was published yesterday. (b) It took the team six months to write. (c) The government requested the study last year. → **c, b, a**.
5. (a) As a result, sales doubled. (b) The company slashed prices. (c) Customers flocked to the stores. → **b, c, a**.

### Common Trap

Choosing the first sentence that "sounds interesting" instead of the one that introduces the topic. Sentences with pronouns ("this", "it", "they") can never open a paragraph.

## Section 8 — Reading Comprehension

### Method

1. Skim the passage first: first sentence of each paragraph = main idea.
2. Read the question, then return to the passage for the exact answer (locate, don't memorize).
3. For "which is NOT mentioned" — scan the options against the passage, find the absent one.
4. For "main idea" — the answer is broader than any detail but narrower than the universe.

### Key Insight

Placement RCs are short and factual. 80% of questions are "what does the passage say" — the answer is a paraphrase of one line. Never answer from general knowledge.

### Worked Examples (passage)

"Urban rooftop gardens cool buildings in summer by shading the roof and releasing moisture. They also absorb rainwater, cutting the load on city drains. Cities like Singapore and New York have offered tax breaks to encourage them."

1. Two benefits mentioned → **cooling buildings; absorbing rainwater**.
2. NOT a benefit → **growing rare flowers** (not mentioned).
3. Why do cities give tax breaks? → **to encourage rooftop gardens**.

### Common Trap

Answering from prior knowledge ("rooftop gardens are pretty") instead of the passage. Every placement RC answer must be traceable to a line.

## Section 9 — Cloze Test (Passage with Blanks)

### Method

1. Read the passage completely before filling any blank.
2. Each blank needs a word that fits its own sentence AND the paragraph's tone.
3. Check grammar: the word after the blank dictates the form (article → noun, etc.).
4. Check connectors: "however/but" = contrast, "therefore/so" = result, "and/also" = addition.

### Key Insight

Connector words unlock cloze tests: identify the relationship (contrast/cause/sequence) and the blank answers itself. Read around the blank ±2 sentences.

### Worked Examples

"The team worked (1) ___ for weeks to meet the deadline. (2) ___, the client changed the requirements. The team had to (3) ___ the entire design."

1. → **hard** (adverb for work).
2. → **However** (contrast: they worked, but client changed things).
3. → **rework/redesign** (result of changed requirements).

### Common Trap

Filling blanks one by one without reading the full passage — the meaning of blank 2 depends on blank 1's context. Always read the whole passage first.

## Section 10 — Idioms & Phrases

### Formula Box

```
Memorize the most common placement idioms:
  Hit the nail on the head = be exactly right
  A blessing in disguise = apparent misfortune that turns out well
  Bite the bullet = face something difficult bravely
  Let the cat out of the bag = reveal a secret
  Once in a blue moon = very rarely
  Beat around the bush = avoid the main point
  The ball is in your court = it's your turn to act
  Burn the midnight oil = work late
  Break the ice = start a conversation
  Cut corners = do something cheaply/wrongly to save time
```

### Key Insight

Idioms are literal-trap targets: the wrong options are literal readings ("to work as a carpenter" for "hit the nail on the head"). Always pick the figurative meaning.

### Worked Examples

1. "Hit the nail on the head" → **be exactly right** (not carpentry).
2. "A blessing in disguise" → **apparent misfortune that turns out well**.
3. "Bite the bullet" → **face a difficult situation bravely**.
4. "Let the cat out of the bag" → **reveal a secret**.
5. "Once in a blue moon" → **very rarely**.

### Common Trap

Literal interpretations. "To rain cats and dogs" is heavy rain, not animals falling. The figurative meaning is always the answer.
