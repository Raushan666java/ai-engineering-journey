# Chapter 10 — Sales Conversations

## Learning Objectives

After this chapter you will be able to:

- Run a 20-minute discovery call with a structure that produces a decision
- Qualify with four questions instead of guessing whether the buyer can buy
- Handle the four objections that end most technical sales, using evidence rather than reassurance
- Write a one-page proposal that closes without a second meeting
- Set a follow-up cadence that is persistent without being annoying
- Recognise the five red flags that predict an unpaid invoice

---

## Theory

### 10.1 The call has one job

A discovery call is not a pitch. Its job is to decide, together, whether there is
a fit and what the next step is. That decision needs three facts:

| fact | why it decides the outcome |
|---|---|
| the cost of the problem | sets the price ceiling and the urgency |
| who decides | determines whether this conversation can close |
| what happens next | converts a pleasant talk into a dated action |

If the call ends without all three, it did not fail politely; it failed
expensively, because you will spend the next three weeks following up on a
conversation that was never going anywhere.

### 10.2 Structure for twenty minutes

| minutes | stage | what you do |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | frame | say what the call is for and how long it takes |
| 2–10 | their situation | ask, do not pitch; take notes visibly |
| 10–14 | cost and impact | put a number on the problem |
| 14–17 | fit | say plainly what you do and do not do |
| 17–20 | next step | propose one specific dated action |

The frame at minute zero matters more than it looks. "I would like to understand
how enquiries work today, then tell you whether I can help, and if not I will say
so — about twenty minutes" removes the buyer's fear of being pitched at, which is
the main reason people avoid calls.

**The listening ratio:** you should speak less than 40% of the time. A technical
seller's instinct is to demonstrate competence by explaining. The buyer
interprets this as not listening, and the price conversation gets harder because
you never learned what the problem costs.

### 10.3 Qualifying with four questions

BANT (budget, authority, need, timeline) is the classic framework and it is
widely criticised for encouraging checkbox interrogation. The four questions
below get the same information conversationally:

| question | what it reveals |
|---|---|
| "What have you already tried?" | whether the pain is real, and what failed |
| "What does it cost you at the moment?" | the price ceiling, in their numbers |
| "Who else is involved in a decision like this?" | authority and the real buying process |
| "If this worked, when would you want it running?" | timeline and urgency, without asking "what's your timeline" |

Each is about the past or the concrete future. None invites a polite lie.

**The hidden question** is the fifth one you ask yourself: does this person have
money and permission? If both answers are unclear after twenty minutes, the
correct next step is not a proposal, it is "let us include the person who owns
the budget".

### 10.4 The four objections

Objections are information. Each of the four below has a specific answer, and
reassurance is not it.

| objection | what it usually means | the answer that works |
|---|---|---|
| **"It's too expensive"** | the value is not established, or the budget is real and small | re-anchor on the cost of the problem, then offer a smaller scope |
| **"I need to think about it"** | an unspoken objection, usually risk or authority | "Of course. What would you need to know to decide?" |
| **"Can you do it cheaper?"** | testing the price, or comparing to a number they have in mind | reduce scope, never the rate; name what the lower price removes |
| **"We tried something like this and it failed"** | a real, specific scar | ask what failed and design around it explicitly in the proposal |

The "I need to think about it" answer is the most valuable line in this chapter,
because it converts a soft no into a named obstacle. It is also the line sellers
are most afraid to say.

**What not to do:** defend your price by listing your qualifications. The buyer
did not ask about you; they asked about the value. Answering a value objection
with a credential objection changes the subject and loses the frame.

### 10.5 The one-page proposal

A proposal exists to be forwarded to someone who was not on the call. That person
has two minutes, so the document has one page and six sections:

```
1  The situation       their problem in their words, two sentences
2  What we will do     three to five bullets, each a deliverable
3  What we will not do the scope boundary, stated plainly
4  Timeline            dates, not durations
5  Price and terms     one number, payment schedule
6  Next step           the single action, with a date
```

Two things sell the proposal: **their words in section 1**, which proves you
listened, and **section 3**, which proves you have done this before. The scope
boundary is what makes a fixed price safe to offer.

Send it within 24 hours. Proposal conversion falls sharply after two days,
because the conversation's energy is perishable and a competitor's proposal will
arrive first.

### 10.6 Follow-up that is persistent without being annoying

A cadence, decided in advance:

| day | action | content |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | send proposal | the document, plus one line of context |
| 2 | check | "Any questions on the scope?" |
| 5 | add value | a relevant example or a scoping clarification |
| 10 | decision request | "Should I hold the slot, or park this?" |
| 21 | close the loop | "Parking this. Reach out when timing changes." |

The day-10 message is the one that produces answers, because it offers a
decision rather than asking for one. "Should I hold the slot or park this" is easy
to answer and cannot be ignored indefinitely.

After day 21, stop. A pipeline of unclosed conversations is not a pipeline; it
is a list of people who are politely avoiding you, and it consumes the attention
you need for people who are not.

### 10.7 Five red flags

These predict non-payment more reliably than any other signal:

| flag | what it sounds like |
|---|---|
| no budget authority | "I'll run it past my partner" with no offer to include them |
| urgency without scope | "we need it by Friday" before anyone has defined it |
| no existing spend | they have never paid for anything in this category |
| scope expansion before signature | "and it should also do X, obviously" |
| refusal of any deposit | "we always pay on completion" |

One flag is a conversation. Three flags is a client who will cost you money. The
correct response is to require payment upfront or decline, and declining is
cheaper than the alternative.

## Examples

### Example 1: A twenty-minute call, written out

```
[0:00] Frame
"You mentioned enquiries after closing. I'd like to understand how it works now
and what it costs you, then tell you whether I can help. If I can't, I'll say so.
About twenty minutes — does that still work?"

[2:00] Situation
"Walk me through what happens when someone messages the gym at nine at night."

[5:00] Past attempts
"What have you tried so far?"          <- listen for the failed chatbot
"What happened with it?"

[8:00] Cost
"Roughly how many enquiries do you think you lose a month?"
"And what's a member worth to you over a year?"

[11:00] Authority
"If you decided to do this, who else would be involved?"

[13:00] Fit
"Here's what I do: [one sentence]. What I don't do is [one sentence].
Based on what you've said, this sounds like [fit / not a fit]."

[16:00] Next step
"I can put a one-page scope and price in front of you by Thursday. If the numbers
work, we start the following Monday. Should I send it to you, or to you and
[partner's name]?"

[19:00] Close
"Thanks — Thursday, then. I'll include the staff-time saving as well, since you
mentioned that."
```

Three details make this script work. The frame at zero removes the fear of being
pitched. The authority question at eleven is asked before the price is discussed,
so the proposal goes to the right person. The close names a date and a person,
which is a decision, not a hope.

### Example 2: Objection handling, timed

| the buyer says | a weak reply | the reply that works |
|---|---|---|
| "25,000 is a lot" | "I can do 20,000" | "Compared with what? You mentioned losing about 25 enquiries a month — at your close rate that's 9,000 a month. The setup pays back in under three months. If the budget is genuinely 15,000, I can do the booking flow without payment collection." |
| "Send me a proposal and I'll think" | "Sure, when suits?" | "Happy to. So the proposal is useful — what would you need to see to say yes?" |
| "Can you do it cheaper?" | "I can knock 10% off" | "I can, by removing the payment integration. That's the week of work. Which matters more to you, collecting fees automatically or the lower price?" |
| "We tried a bot and it was useless" | "Ours is much better" | "What did it fail at? ... Right, it couldn't take payments, so people dropped off. That's the part I'd build first, and it's why I price the payment flow separately." |
| "Let me discuss with my partner" | "No problem, take your time" | "Of course. Would it help if I joined a ten-minute call with them so they can ask me directly?" |

Every strong reply does one of three things: it re-anchors on the buyer's own
number, it isolates the real objection, or it removes scope instead of price.
None of them defends the seller's competence, because the buyer never attacked it.

## Summary

- A discovery call exists to establish the cost of the problem, who decides, and the next dated action.
- Framing the call at minute zero removes the buyer's main reason to avoid it.
- Speaking less than 40% of the time is the discipline that makes the price conversation possible.
- Four conversational questions replace checkbox qualification and are harder to answer politely.
- Each of the four common objections has a specific answer, and reassurance is never it.
- A one-page proposal in the buyer's own words, with an explicit scope boundary, closes without a second meeting.
- A five-step follow-up cadence ending at day 21 keeps the pipeline honest.
- Three red flags mean require payment upfront or decline.

## Exercises

### Review Questions

1. Why is a discovery call's success measured by the next dated action rather than by enthusiasm?
2. What does "I need to think about it" usually mean, and what question converts it into information?
3. Why does answering a price objection with your qualifications fail?
4. What are the two sections of a proposal that do the most selling, and why?
5. Why stop following up at day 21?

### Application Problems

1. Write your own 20-minute call script with timestamps and the exact words for the frame and the close.
2. Take the five objections and write your real answer to each, using a number from your own offer.
3. Draft a one-page proposal for a plausible client, including the "what we will not do" section.

### Challenge Problem

A prospect with clear budget and urgency refuses any deposit and wants a fixed price for undefined scope. Write the exact response that keeps the deal alive without accepting the risk, including the alternative structure you would offer and the point at which you would walk away.
