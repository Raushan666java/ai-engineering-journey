Chapter 6 — Content Engine and SEO

Learning Objectives​

After this chapter you will be able to:

  • Classify any query into one of four search intents and predict what the searcher will do next
  • Build a keyword list without a paid tool, using autocomplete and question mining
  • Structure a topic cluster with one pillar page and supporting posts
  • Apply the one-post-one-question rule so every piece has a single job
  • Produce a 12-week content calendar with publishable titles and a repurposing plan
  • Measure content honestly, including the months where nothing appears to happen

Theory​

6.1 Four intents, four different pages​

Search intent is what the person wants to do next. Getting it wrong is the most common reason good content ranks and never converts.

intentexample querysearcher wantsthe page that serves it
informational"what is retrieval augmented generation"to understanda guide, no offer
navigational"chroma docs"a specific sitenot for you
commercial"best vector database for small teams"comparison before decidinga comparison with a recommendation
transactional"hire fastapi developer india"to contact someonea service page with price and proof

The trap: a technical writer produces excellent informational content and then wonders why nobody hires them. Informational readers are not buyers yet. The conversion path is informational → commercial → transactional, and it requires pages at each stage. Most developer sites have only the first.

A practical ratio for a solo seller with limited time: one informational post for every three commercial or transactional pages, because the commercial pages are what actually produce enquiries and they are much faster to write.

6.2 Keyword research without a paid tool​

Five free sources, in order of usefulness:

1. Google autocomplete. Type your problem phrase and record what completes. It reflects real query volume, because it is generated from queries.

2. People Also Ask. The question boxes in results are literal questions with demand behind them, and each one is a publishable section heading.

3. Your own Search Console. After thirty days, this shows the queries you already appear for. It finds accidental wins, which are the cheapest content to double down on.

4. Community titles. Sort a forum or subreddit by top posts of the year. Every title that repeats is a validated question. This is often more useful than keyword tools for niche technical audiences, because it captures the exact phrasing buyers use.

5. Competitor sitemaps. A competitor's blog index is a list of topics that already work for them. Do not copy; find what they missed.

What to record for each candidate: the query, its intent, whether you can answer it better than what currently ranks, and whether the answer naturally leads to your offer.

The last criterion filters hardest. A query you can rank for that has no path to your offer is a hobby.

6.3 Topic clusters​

A cluster is one pillar page covering a broad problem, plus supporting pages covering each sub-problem, each linking back to the pillar.

Pillar:  "WhatsApp automation for gyms: the complete setup"
   |
   +-- "How to answer gym enquiries after closing time"
   +-- "WhatsApp Business API vs personal WhatsApp for a gym"
   +-- "How much does a gym management system cost in India"
   +-- "How to reduce missed appointments at a clinic"

The cluster works for three reasons: it signals topical depth to search engines, it creates internal links that pass authority, and it gives you one coherent thing to write about for three months instead of inventing a topic each week.

6.4 One post, one question​

Every piece answers exactly one question, stated as its title. If a draft answers three questions, split it into three.

Benefits: the title becomes obvious, the structure becomes the answer, the internal links point at siblings rather than rambling, and the reader can decide in two seconds whether to stay.

The test: can you say what the reader will be able to do after reading, in one sentence? If not, the piece has no job.

6.5 Cadence and repurposing​

For a working developer, one substantial post per week is realistic and sufficient. Frequency matters less than consistency and depth. Nine good posts in nine weeks beats twenty thin posts in one week followed by silence.

Repurposing is where the leverage is, because the research is already paid for:

outputderived fromtime
long post (1,500–2,500 words)research + a real example4–6 hours
LinkedIn post (200 words)the strongest paragraph10 minutes
thread (8 posts)the section headings20 minutes
short video (3–5 min)walk through the example30 minutes
email to listthe summary plus one link5 minutes

One week of work produces five assets. This is the only way a solo seller publishes consistently while also doing client work.

6.6 Measuring content without lying to yourself​

metricwhat it tells youwhat it does not
impressionsthe title is being shownwhether anyone cares
click-through ratethe title and snippet workwhether the page delivers
engaged timethe page deliverswhether they buy
assisted conversionscontent contributeswhich piece caused the sale
direct enquiriessomeone read and contactednothing, this is the real one

The honest baseline: SEO takes three to six months to produce meaningful traffic, and the first ninety days look like failure. Content is a bet on compounding, and it should be run alongside a fast channel rather than instead of one. A seller with no clients who spends month one on SEO has chosen the slowest available path and will run out of money before it pays.

The correct sequencing: fast channel for cash, content for the asset. Four hours a week on content while the other channels run.

6.7 Technical basics that are actually required​

None of these are optional, and all are quick:

elementrule
title tag50–60 characters, contains the query, no clickbait
meta description140–160 characters, states the answer, not a teaser
H1one per page, matches the title tag closely
internal linksevery post links to the pillar and to one sibling
URLshort, lowercase, hyphens, no dates
page speedunder 2.5s largest contentful paint, check with PageSpeed Insights
mobileassume the majority of readers are on a phone
schemaArticle or FAQ markup where genuine

Google's quality guidance, summarised as E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness, trust), is worth reading once. For a technical author the cheapest way to demonstrate experience is to include something only someone who did the work could write: the error you hit, the number you measured, the version that broke. Generic explanation is abundant; specific experience is not.

Examples​

Example 1: Turning raw queries into a content plan​

"""Classify queries by intent and decide what to publish for each.

The rule applied here: informational queries get one post each, and every three
informational posts must be paired with one commercial page, because commercial
pages are what produce enquiries.
"""
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass

TRANSACTIONAL = re.compile(r"\b(hire|cost|price|pricing|agency|company|services|near me|consultant)\b")
COMMERCIAL = re.compile(r"\b(best|vs|versus|compare|alternatives|review|which)\b")


def intent(q: str) -> str:
    """Classification is deliberately blunt and order-dependent.

    A bare noun phrase ("whatsapp automation for gyms") has no purchase signal,
    so it is research, not a transaction. Only an explicit money or comparison
    word moves a query into the later stages.
    """
    q = q.lower().strip()
    if TRANSACTIONAL.search(q):
        return "transactional"
    if COMMERCIAL.search(q):
        return "commercial"
    return "informational"


queries = [
    "whatsapp automation for gyms",
    "how to answer gym enquiries after closing",
    "best whatsapp api for small business india",
    "how much does a gym management system cost",
    "hire laravel developer patna",
    "whatsapp business api vs personal whatsapp",
    "gym software price india",
]

plan = []
for q in queries:
    i = intent(q)
    action = {
        "transactional": "service page with price and proof",
        "commercial": "comparison page with a recommendation",
        "informational": "post answering exactly this question",
    }[i]
    plan.append((i, q, action))
    print(f"{i:<14} {q:<48} -> {action}")

counts = {}
for i, _, _ in plan:
    counts[i] = counts.get(i, 0) + 1
print("\nmix:", counts)

Expected output:

informational  whatsapp automation for gyms                       -> post answering exactly this question
informational  how to answer gym enquiries after closing          -> post answering exactly this question
commercial     best whatsapp api for small business india         -> comparison page with a recommendation
transactional  how much does a gym management system cost         -> service page with price and proof
transactional  hire laravel developer patna                       -> service page with price and proof
commercial     whatsapp business api vs personal whatsapp         -> comparison page with a recommendation
transactional  gym software price india                           -> service page with price and proof

mix: {'informational': 2, 'commercial': 2, 'transactional': 3}

Now the mix is visible: two informational, two commercial, three transactional. The chapter's ratio asks for one informational per three commercial pages, so this list is heavier on information than it should be, and the plan should convert the two informational queries into one post and add two more commercial pages instead.

That is the entire value of classifying: it makes an imbalance visible before you spend a month writing the wrong things.

Summary​

  • Intent determines everything: informational readers are not buyers, and a site with only informational pages produces no enquiries.
  • Free research sources (autocomplete, People Also Ask, Search Console, community titles, competitor sitemaps) are sufficient to start.
  • A topic cluster of one pillar plus supporting posts signals depth and creates internal linking.
  • Every post answers exactly one question, stated in its title.
  • One deep post per week, repurposed into five assets, is the realistic cadence for a working developer.
  • SEO takes three to six months, so it must run alongside a fast channel rather than instead of one.
  • Technical basics (title, description, H1, internal links, speed, mobile) are quick and non-optional.

Exercises​

Review Questions​

  1. Why does ranking for an informational query usually fail to produce clients?
  2. What is the difference between a pillar page and a commercial page, in terms of what the reader does next?
  3. Why is community post titles often better research than a keyword tool for technical niches?
  4. State the one-post-one-question rule and the test that verifies a draft obeys it.
  5. Why must content run alongside a fast channel rather than replace it?

Application Problems​

  1. Collect twenty real queries from autocomplete and People Also Ask for your problem area. Classify each by intent and compute your mix.
  2. Design one topic cluster: a pillar title, six supporting titles, and the internal links between them.
  3. Write the twelve-week calendar for your cluster, marking which rows are informational, commercial, transactional and proof.

Challenge Problem​

You have four hours a week and a working job. Design a content plan that produces three commercial pages within six weeks without writing three long posts, by repurposing the same underlying research into a comparison, a pricing page and an objections page. Explain what each page must contain to be genuinely useful rather than thin.
